Abstract

8

Background: Multivariate calibrations of NIR and MIR spectra need costly reference

9

analyses, and analysts must decide from a small pilot set whether more samples will pay off.

10

Learning-curve extrapolation is used for this decision, but it is unknown which part of a

11

learning curve a pilot can determine.

12

13

14

15

17

alone. A computable compatible set of curves consistent with the pilot contained the true

20

21

1. Introduction

32

Multivariate calibration of near-infrared (NIR), mid-infrared (MIR) and visible–near-infrared

33

(VisNIR) spectra is the workhorse of rapid quantitative analysis in agriculture, food, fuels,

34

pharmaceuticals and soil science [1,2]. Once a calibration exists, a measurement costs

35

seconds; building it does not. Every calibration sample needs a reference value from a slower

36

and more expensive primary method — oven drying, combustion analysis, wet chemistry or

37

chromatographic assay — and this cost usually dominates the development budget. Analysts

38

therefore face a recurring planning question before most of the reference work is done: how

39

many calibration samples are needed to reach a target prediction error, and is that target

40

achievable at all with a linear calibration? Current guidance is largely heuristic, for example

41

minimum sample numbers expressed relative to model complexity, or experience from related

42

applications. Formal minimum-sample-size criteria developed for low-dimensional clinical

43

prediction models, which limit overfitting and optimism under linear-Gaussian assumptions,

44

have only recently been transferred to chemometrics [3,4]. They bound overfitting at a given

45

size but do not predict how the error of a high-dimensional spectral calibration decreases as

46

samples are added. Empirical studies in soil spectroscopy show that accuracy keeps
2

47

improving well beyond a few hundred samples before levelling off [5–7], but such curves are

48

observed only after the data have been collected.

49

A learning curve — prediction error as a function of calibration size — answers the planning

50

question if it can be forecast early. The established approach, introduced to analytical

51

chemistry for Raman classification by Ali et al. [8] and rooted in earlier work on microarray

52

and text classification [9,10], sub-samples a small pilot data set, fits a parametric curve such

53

as an inverse power law and extrapolates it. Its weakness is structural: the curve shape is

54

assumed rather than derived, and a small pilot constrains it only near the pilot size, so long

55

extrapolations are fragile [11]. Learning-curve extrapolation is also an active topic in

56

automated machine learning, where curves over training epochs are extrapolated with learned

57

priors [12], and empirical scaling laws are extrapolated in deep learning [13,14]; none of these

58

approaches uses the structure of spectroscopic data.

59

Statistical learning theory offers such structure. For ridge regression in high dimensions,

60

“deterministic-equivalent” formulas derived with random-matrix theory predict the expected

61

test error from two ingredients: the eigenvalue spectrum of the covariance of the inputs and

62

the way the target signal is distributed across the corresponding eigenvectors [15–18]. The

63

same results explain why learning curves of kernel methods differ between tasks (“spectral

64

bias” and “task–model alignment”) [19–22]. These analyses are, however, omniscient: they

65

explain a learning curve when the full data are available. Related estimators such as

66

generalised cross-validation (GCV) [23] and the kernel-alignment risk estimator [24,25]

67

estimate the risk at the current sample size, not at a larger one. Wei et al. [25] used these

68

estimators to predict how the error of trained neural representations scales with the number of

69

samples, and Beleites et al. [26] showed in this journal that the test sets of small classification

70

studies are too small to estimate performance reliably, let alone its future improvement. No

71

study has asked which part of a calibration learning curve a pilot set can determine at all. The
3

72

question matters because planning methods are judged by how well they extrapolate, and a

73

method that extrapolates well on one application can fail on another for reasons that remain

74

invisible without such an analysis. Spectroscopy is a good setting in which to answer it. Ridge

75

regression, principal-component regression and partial least squares (PLS) are closely related

76

shrinkage estimators [27–29], and unlabelled spectra are cheap, so the covariance spectrum

77

can be estimated accurately long before many reference values exist.

78

This paper makes three contributions. (i) An observability limit. We prove that a pilot of n0

79

samples observes the bias function of a ridge calibration only above an effective-

80

regularisation horizon set by n0 and the eigen-spectrum; target signal carried by spectral

81

modes below that horizon is indistinguishable from irreducible noise in the pilot, yet it is

82

learned when the calibration grows (Proposition 1). The gain from adding samples is therefore

83

not identifiable from a pilot alone, and every forecaster, including power-law extrapolation

84

and the no-improvement rule, implicitly supplies a prior. The corollary is a computable, prior-

85

free compatible set of learning curves consistent with the pilot, and an information fraction

86

that measures how much the pilot adds to the prior about the future gain. (ii) Bayesian

87

regularisation-path matching (B-RPM). Instead of assuming a curve shape, B-RPM places

88

a transferable prior on two structural parameters of the deterministic-equivalent risk, the

89

alignment exponent and the noise fraction, learned from previous calibrations of other

90

materials. It combines this prior with the pilot’s exact leave-one-group-out regularisation path

91

and the eigen-spectrum of the task’s own unlabelled spectra, and returns a posterior-predictive

92

learning curve in a fraction of a second. (iii) A leakage-free benchmark on 38 calibration

93

tasks from six public sources (soils, mango fruit, diesel fuel, pharmaceutical tablets, meat and

94

designed mixtures). It includes a pre-registered first design, a pre-specified evaluation of B-

95

RPM (hypotheses fixed before results were seen), a controlled simulation, group-disjoint

96

repeated splits and the strictest transfer test available, in which priors come from a different
4

97

application family. The main results are as follows. The theory reproduces ridge and PLS

98

learning curves. The compatible set contains the true curve in about nine of ten cases, but it is

99

wide at long horizons. B-RPM predicts gains better than power-law extrapolation and the no-

100

improvement rule and keeps its accuracy when its prior comes from a different application

101

family, whereas a transferred empirical slope does not. No forecaster beats the pilot’s own

102

cross-validated error on the RMSEP level, and a second batch of samples improves sample-

103

size decisions more than any choice of forecaster.

240

241

242

3.2. Pre-processing and leakage control

243

Reflectance was converted to absorbance, and all spectra were standard-normal-variate (SNV)

244

transformed [38] after wavelength sub-sampling to 94–216 variables (Table 1). Both

245

operations are sample-wise, so no information passes between calibration and test samples.

246

Replicate scans of the same physical sample were averaged before any split. This applies to

247

the two cheek scans of each mango fruit (fruit identity was reconstructed from metadata and

248

validated by spectral distance), to duplicate KSSL scans, and to the multi-instrument

249

NeoSpectra measurements, for which the instrument-averaged spectrum was used. Soil layers

250

from the same site or profile were assigned to the same group. Every split is group-disjoint,

251

and every cross-validation, inside pilots and inside ground-truth fits, leaves out whole groups

252

[39]. Unlabelled spectra used by RPM and B-RPM were taken only from the calibration pool,

253

never from the test set. Supplementary Note 3 documents the full leakage audit.

254

3.3. Ground-truth learning curves

255

For each task, a group-disjoint test set (25% of samples, at most 3000) was held out using a

256

seed derived from the task name; the remaining samples form the pool. Tasks with fewer than

257

1000 samples (fuel, tablets, meat, mixtures) were evaluated over ten independent group-

258

disjoint outer splits, so that their ground truth does not rest on one small test set. For

259

n ∈ {25 , 50 ,100 , 200 , 400 , 800 ,1600 , 3200 , 6400 } (up to the pool size), n pool samples were

260

drawn at random, and two calibrations were fitted. The first was ridge, with the penalty
12

261

chosen by exact LOGO cross-validation over a 53-point grid. The second was PLS1 (kernel

262

algorithm [40]), with 1–30 latent variables chosen by grouped 10-fold cross-validation. The

263

test MSE of each was recorded. The expected curve was estimated from 30 (n ≤ 400), 16 (

264

n ≤ 1600) or 8 repetitions.

265

4.2. Analyte signal extends into weak spectral modes, and the noise floor

366

sets the slope

367

The large-sample fits also estimate the alignment exponent r, which describes how the analyte

368

signal is distributed over the eigenvalues of the spectral covariance ( s k ∝ λ k ). In all 38 tasks, r

369

370

372

373

374

375

information in better-resolved, high-variance features. Because weak modes become learnable

376

only gradually, learning curves keep improving over a wide range of n.

469

4.6. Uncertainty and planning decisions

497

The posterior-predictive bands of B-RPM were too narrow. Nominal 80% and 95% bands

498

covered the truth in 55% and 73% of cases (task averages; median width factor 1.7), so HB3

499

was not met, and coverage was lowest for the designed mixtures (11%). The bands contain the

500

uncertainty of the pilot’s regularisation path but not the difference between a pilot’s cross-

501

validated error and the error of a calibration trained on it, which our earlier analysis identified

502

as the dominant source of level error. A post-hoc recalibration of the predictive spread,

503

estimated leave-one-source-out from the standardised errors of the other sources, restored

504

nominal coverage (79% and 95%; 80–85% across pilot sizes; soil 89%, mixtures 26%) at a

505

median width factor of 2.4. IPL3 reached similar coverage only with bands that spanned a

506

median factor of 13.8 (Supplementary Table S10). For the probabilistic decision ‘will an

507

eightfold larger calibration set reduce RMSEP by at least 15%?’, no method beat the base rate

508

(Brier score 0.290 for B-RPM, 0.274 for FLAT, 0.294 for SLOPE-T and 0.247 for the

509

climatological forecast; Supplementary Table S11). This is consistent with Proposition 1.

510

Such decisions turn on precisely the gain that a 25–200-sample pilot cannot determine.

511

We therefore tested the staged design that Proposition 1 suggests, using the existing forecasts

512

(Supplementary Table S15). An analyst with an absolute RMSEP target and a budget of N =

513

400 or 800 reference samples asks whether the target is reachable. Targets were set 15%

514

above and 15% below the true RMSEP at N . Deciding from a pilot of N /8 samples was

515

correct in 68% (N = 400) and 76% (N = 800) of cases with B-RPM, 74% and 76% with

516

FLAT, and 63% and 67% with IPL3. Taking a second batch first, i.e. deciding from N / 4

517

samples, raised the accuracy of every method by 4–10 percentage points (B-RPM 78% and

518

84%; FLAT 80% and 86%). The error of a B-RPM forecast for a fixed size of 800 samples

519

fell from 0.244 with a 50-sample pilot to 0.158 with 100 and 0.121 with 200 (Supplementary

520

Table S14). The methods failed in characteristic directions. FLAT declared reachable targets
25

521

unreachable (correct for only 55% of reachable targets), and SLOPE-T declared unreachable

522

targets reachable (correct for 33% of unreachable ones). B-RPM was the most balanced (80%

523

and 64%). Gating the second batch on the compatible set did not save reference analyses: at

524

this ±15% resolution, the set excluded the target in only 7% of cases, so a second batch was

525

needed almost always. For decisions this fine, a 25–100-sample pilot cannot replace a second

526

batch.

4.7. Robustness, distribution shift and ablation

528

On six representative tasks, B-RPM was evaluated with raw-absorbance and first-derivative

529

spectra, Kennard–Stone-selected pilots and noisier reference values, and on mango under a

530

new-season and an orchard-population shift (Supplementary Table S20). With raw and first-

531

derivative spectra its mean absolute log errors were 0.289 and 0.319 (FLAT 0.215 and 0.272;

532

IPL3 0.467 and 0.398). With added reference noise they were 0.143 (FLAT 0.117). In these

533

scenarios B-RPM was therefore less accurate than the no-improvement rule, though more

534

accurate than curve extrapolation; on six tasks the pilot’s own error remains the most robust

535

single number. Kennard–Stone pilots made every anchored forecast pessimistic (B-RPM bias

536

+0.084, FLAT +0.233), because a pilot that spans the extremes of the spectral space has a

537

larger cross-validated error than a random sample. Under the season shift, B-RPM became

538

clearly optimistic (bias -0.502, against -0.255 for FLAT and -1.040 for IPL3): it forecast the

539

improvement expected within the calibration seasons, which the new season did not deliver.

540

No method anticipated a shift, and pilots must be drawn at random from the population in

541

which the calibration will be used. In the controlled simulation (Supplementary Table S21),

542

B-RPM with the real-data prior (median r = 0.55) was the most accurate method when the

543

true alignment was close to that prior (r=0.5: 0.224 vs 0.408 for FLAT, 0.346 for RPM and

544

0.251 for IPL3; with a flat prior 0.400). When the prior was wrong (r=2), it lost to the no-

545

improvement rule (0.362 vs 0.095). This is the cost of importing information that Proposition
26

546

1 makes unavoidable. The pre-registered ablations (Supplementary Table S19) showed that

547

freeing the alignment exponent without a prior is the most damaging choice (Section 2.4).

4.8. Comparison with previous work and practical recommendations

549

Learning-curve extrapolation for sample-size planning in analytical spectroscopy [8] and in

550

classification more generally [9,10,26] has relied on the assumption that the curve’s shape can

551

be read from a pilot. Our results explain why this works for short extrapolations and fails for

552

long ones: the shape below the observability horizon is not in the pilot. The same reasoning

553

applies to the minimum-sample-size criteria transferred from clinical prediction modelling

554

[3,4], which control overfitting at a given size but do not predict the gain from more samples.

555

Recent work in this journal has emphasised validation design [44–46], replicate-based error

556

structure [47], calibration-set reduction [48], prediction uncertainty [49] and benchmarks of

557

modern regressors on many NIR data sets [50]. Its practical advice on multivariate calibration

558

[51] fits our observations. The reference-method error sets a floor, the floor sets the slope, and

559

learning curves for linear calibrations are shallow. The present work adds the planning side: a

560

statement of what the pilot can and cannot tell, and a forecaster whose prior information is

561

explicit, transferable and measurable.

562

We therefore recommend the following. (i) Do not extrapolate learning curves with power-

563

law, exponential or logarithmic fits to a small pilot. (ii) Treat the pilot’s cross-validated error

564

as the best single estimate of the current error, and plan decisions that depend on the gain as

565

staged decisions: a second batch of the same size improved every method’s decisions more

566

than any choice of forecaster did. (iii) Report the compatible set with any forecast, as an

567

honest statement of what the pilot allows. (iv) When a gain forecast is needed before the

568

second batch, prefer a forecaster with an explicit structural prior (B-RPM) over a transferred

569

slope whenever the previous calibrations come from other materials, and recalibrate its bands

570

on previous calibrations. (v) Draw pilots at random from the target population. A space-filling
27

571

selection such as Kennard–Stone remains the right choice for building the final calibration,

572

but not for forecasting.

5. Limitations

574

1.

Linear calibrations. The theory describes ridge regression and tracked PLS closely

575

(Section 4.1). Non-linear and local calibration methods may keep improving after

576

linear models saturate, and their observability limits are not covered.

577

2.

r

Parametric family. The compatible set and B-RPM use the power-law family s k ∝ λ k .

578

Other families could give wider sets. The coverage of about 90% on real data and 99%

579

in simulation suggests that the family is adequate for these spectra, but it is not

580

guaranteed for other materials.

581

3.

Pre-specified hypotheses not met. B-RPM’s raw bands under-covered (HB3). Its

582

level error was 0.02 above the no-improvement rule, so non-inferiority (HB2) was not

583

shown. Under cross-family transfer, its gain error equalled that of the transferred slope

584

(HB1). The recalibrated bands are post hoc.

585

4.

Random sampling from a stable population. Kennard–Stone pilots and genuine

586

shifts bias every anchored forecast (Section 4.7). In the robustness scenarios, B-RPM

587

was less accurate than the no-improvement rule, and under a season shift it was

588

optimistic.

589

5.

Data sets. 23 of the 38 tasks are soil calibrations. The fruit, fuel, pharmaceutical, meat

590

and mixture sets are smaller and allow only short extrapolations, and the designed

591

mixtures violate the random-sampling premise of the theory.

592

6. Conclusions

593

A small pilot set determines a calibration’s error curve only above an observability horizon

594

set by its size and the eigen-spectrum of the spectra. Below the horizon, analyte signal and

595

irreducible noise cannot be told apart, so the gain from more reference samples must come

596

from prior knowledge. This explains the optimism of power-law extrapolation and the

597

strength of the no-improvement rule. The compatible set makes the limit visible for every

598

pilot. B-RPM turns the unavoidable prior into an explicit one; it predicted gains better than

599

power-law extrapolation and the no-improvement rule and, unlike a transferred slope, kept its

600

accuracy across application families, but it did not beat the pilot’s own error on the RMSEP

601

level. For planning, the practical consequence is a staged design. Use the pilot’s error as the

602

current estimate and a structural forecast with its compatible set as a guide, and take a second

603

batch before committing the full reference budget.
