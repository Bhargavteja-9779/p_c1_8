# 1. Introduction

Multivariate calibration of near-infrared (NIR), mid-infrared (MIR) and visible–near-infrared (VisNIR) spectra is the workhorse of rapid quantitative analysis in agriculture, food, fuels, pharmaceuticals and soil science [@wold2001; @westad2015]. Once a calibration exists, a measurement costs seconds; building it does not. Every calibration sample needs a reference value from a slower and more expensive primary method — oven drying, combustion analysis, wet chemistry or chromatographic assay — and this cost usually dominates the development budget. Analysts therefore face a recurring planning question before most of the reference work is done: *how many calibration samples are needed to reach a target prediction error, and is that target achievable at all with a linear calibration?* Current guidance is largely heuristic, for example minimum sample numbers expressed relative to model complexity, or experience from related applications. Formal minimum-sample-size criteria developed for low-dimensional clinical prediction models, which limit overfitting and optimism under linear-Gaussian assumptions, have only recently been transferred to chemometrics [@riley2020; @konstantinidis2025]. They bound overfitting at a given size but do not predict how the error of a high-dimensional spectral calibration decreases as samples are added. Empirical studies in soil spectroscopy show that accuracy keeps improving well beyond a few hundred samples before levelling off [@ng2020; @kuang2014; @luca2017], but such curves are observed only after the data have been collected.

A learning curve — prediction error as a function of calibration size — answers the planning question if it can be forecast early. The established approach, introduced to analytical chemistry for Raman classification by Ali et al. [@ali2018] and rooted in earlier work on microarray and text classification [@mukherjee2003; @figueroa2012], sub-samples a small pilot data set, fits a parametric curve such as an inverse power law and extrapolates it. Its weakness is structural: the curve shape is assumed rather than derived, and a small pilot constrains it only near the pilot size, so long extrapolations are fragile [@viering2023]. Learning-curve extrapolation is also an active topic in automated machine learning, where curves over training epochs are extrapolated with learned priors [@adriaensen2023], and empirical scaling laws are extrapolated in deep learning [@hestness2017; @kaplan2020]; none of these approaches uses the structure of spectroscopic data.

Statistical learning theory offers such structure. For ridge regression in high dimensions, "deterministic-equivalent" formulas derived with random-matrix theory predict the expected test error from two ingredients: the eigenvalue spectrum of the covariance of the inputs and the way the target signal is distributed across the corresponding eigenvectors [@dobriban2018; @hastie2022; @bach2024; @atanasov2024]. The same results explain why learning curves of kernel methods differ between tasks ("spectral bias" and "task–model alignment") [@bordelon2020; @canatar2021; @spigler2020; @loureiro2022]. These analyses are, however, *omniscient*: they explain a learning curve when the full data are available. Related estimators such as generalised cross-validation (GCV) [@craven1979] and the kernel-alignment risk estimator [@jacot2020; @wei2022] estimate the risk at the *current* sample size, not at a larger one. Wei et al. [@wei2022] used these estimators to predict how the error of trained neural representations scales with the number of samples, and Beleites et al. [@beleites2013] showed in this journal that the test sets of small classification studies are too small to estimate performance reliably, let alone its future improvement. No study has asked which part of a calibration learning curve a pilot set can determine at all. The question matters because planning methods are judged by how well they extrapolate, and a method that extrapolates well on one application can fail on another for reasons that remain invisible without such an analysis. Spectroscopy is a good setting in which to answer it. Ridge regression, principal-component regression and partial least squares (PLS) are closely related shrinkage estimators [@frank1993; @hoerl1970; @cook2019], and unlabelled spectra are cheap, so the covariance spectrum can be estimated accurately long before many reference values exist.

This paper makes three contributions. (i) **An observability limit.** We prove that a pilot of $n_0$ samples observes the bias function of a ridge calibration only above an effective-regularisation horizon set by $n_0$ and the eigen-spectrum; target signal carried by spectral modes below that horizon is indistinguishable from irreducible noise in the pilot, yet it is learned when the calibration grows (Proposition 1). The gain from adding samples is therefore not identifiable from a pilot alone, and every forecaster, including power-law extrapolation and the no-improvement rule, implicitly supplies a prior. The corollary is a computable, prior-free *compatible set* of learning curves consistent with the pilot, and an *information fraction* that measures how much the pilot adds to the prior about the future gain. (ii) **Bayesian regularisation-path matching (B-RPM).** Instead of assuming a curve shape, B-RPM places a transferable prior on two structural parameters of the deterministic-equivalent risk, the alignment exponent and the noise fraction, learned from previous calibrations of other materials. It combines this prior with the pilot's exact leave-one-group-out regularisation path and the eigen-spectrum of the task's own unlabelled spectra, and returns a posterior-predictive learning curve in a fraction of a second. (iii) **A leakage-free benchmark on {{n_tasks}} calibration tasks** from eight data sources (soils, mango fruit, diesel fuel, pharmaceutical tablets, meat and designed mixtures). It includes a pre-specified first design, a pre-specified evaluation of B-RPM (hypotheses fixed before results were seen), a controlled simulation, group-disjoint repeated splits and a transfer test in which priors come from a different application family. The main results are as follows. The theory reproduces ridge and PLS learning curves. The compatible set contains the true curve in about nine of ten cases, but it is wide at long horizons. B-RPM predicts gains better than the no-improvement rule and RPM and, on the soil tasks, keeps its accuracy when its prior comes from other application families, whereas a transferred empirical slope does not. No forecaster beats the pilot's own cross-validated error on the RMSEP level, and a second batch of samples improves sample-size decisions more than any choice of forecaster.

# 2. Theory

## 2.1. Setting and notation

Let $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ denote a spectrum after sample-wise pre-processing and $y$ the reference value. A ridge calibration with an unpenalised intercept is fitted to $n$ samples,

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mu}=\arg\min_{a,\boldsymbol\beta}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-a-\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta\right)^2+\mu\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^{2}, \tag{1}
$$

with penalty $\mu>0$ per sample. Let $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\sum_{k}\lambda_k\mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}$ be the population covariance of the spectra ($\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots$), $\boldsymbol\beta^{*}$ the coefficient vector of the best linear predictor, $s_k=\lambda_k(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta^{*})^{2}$ the target variance carried by mode $k$, and $\sigma^{2}$ the residual variance, which comprises reference-method error and any non-linearity that a linear calibration cannot capture.

## 2.2. Deterministic-equivalent risk

Random-matrix theory shows that a ridge model trained on $n$ samples behaves, in expectation, like a population ridge with a larger *effective* penalty $\kappa$, the unique root of [@dobriban2018; @hastie2022; @atanasov2024]

$$
\kappa\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}\frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k+\kappa}\right)=\mu . \tag{2}
$$

Modes with $\lambda_k\gg\kappa$ are learnt; modes with $\lambda_k\ll\kappa$ are not. The expected mean-squared error on new samples (including their reference error) is then

$$
E(n,\mu)=\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{N(\kappa)}{1-\mathrm{df}_2(\kappa)/n},\qquad
N(\kappa)=\sigma^{2}+\sum_{k}s_k\left(\frac{\kappa}{\lambda_k+\kappa}\right)^{2},\qquad
\mathrm{df}_2(\kappa)=\sum_{k}\left(\frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k+\kappa}\right)^{2}, \tag{3}
$$

where the factor $1+1/n$ accounts for estimating the intercept. A calibration whose penalty is tuned by cross-validation is approximated by the minimum over the penalty grid, $E^{*}(n)=\min_{\mu}E(n,\mu)$. Eq. (3) makes the mechanism of a learning curve explicit. As $n$ grows, the attainable $\kappa$ decreases, and the target power in the newly learnable modes is converted into accuracy. The curve therefore keeps improving as long as $s_k$ is non-negligible for modes with $\lambda_k$ between successive values of $\kappa$, and it saturates at $\sigma^{2}$.

## 2.3. Regularisation-path matching

Consider a labelled pilot of $n_0$ samples and a set of unlabelled spectra from the same population. Three observations make forecasting possible.

1. The eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ in Eqs. (2)–(3) depend only on spectra and are estimated from the unlabelled set.
2. The exact leave-one-group-out (LOGO) error of ridge on the pilot, $e(\mu_j)$, is available in closed form for every penalty of a grid. For a linear smoother, the deleted residuals are $\mathbf{r}_g=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{gg})^{-1}\mathbf{e}_g$, where $\mathbf{H}$ is the hat matrix and $g$ indexes groups of samples that must be held out together.
3. By Eq. (3), $e(\mu_j)$ estimates $E(n_0,\mu_j)$, which depends on $\mu_j$ only through $\kappa_j=\kappa(n_0,\mu_j)$ and a known prefactor. The pilot's regularisation path therefore observes the bias function $N(\kappa)$ on $\kappa\ge\kappa_{\min}(n_0)$:

$$
N(\kappa_j)\approx e(\mu_j)\,\frac{1-\mathrm{df}_2(\kappa_j)/n_{0}}{1+1/n_{0}} . \tag{4}
$$

RPM represents the target-power spectrum by $s_k=\tau^{2}(\lambda_k/\lambda_1)^{r}$ with $r=1$ (an isotropic prior on the coefficients, under which ridge is the Bayes estimator). It fits $\boldsymbol\theta=(\log\tau^{2},\log\sigma^{2})$ by least squares in log space over the path points with $\mathrm{df}_2/n_0\le 0.8$:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_{j} w_j\left[\log e(\mu_j)-\log E_{\boldsymbol\theta}(n_0,\mu_j)\right]^{2}, \tag{5}
$$

with $w_j=1$ in the pre-specified version (RPM). In the refined version (RPM-w; Section 3.6), $w_j$ is the inverse of the delta-method variance of $\log e(\mu_j)$, estimated from the pilot's own squared LOGO residuals. The forecast is $\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}(n)=\{\min_{\mu}E_{\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}}(n,\mu)\}^{1/2}$ for any $n$. Uncertainty bands are obtained with a Bayesian bootstrap of the pilot [@rubin1981]: Dirichlet weights enter the weighted ridge fit and the weighted LOGO path, Eq. (5) is refitted, and the forecasts are recomputed.

## 2.4. What a pilot set can identify: the observability horizon

Write the deterministic-equivalent risk as $E(n,\mu)=P(n,\kappa)\,N(\kappa)$ with $\kappa=\kappa(n,\mu)$ (Eqs. 1–4). For a pilot of $n_0<\mathrm{rank}(\Sigma)$ samples, $\kappa(n_0,\mu)$ decreases to a strictly positive limit $\kappa_0=\lim_{\mu\to0}\kappa(n_0,\mu)$, the root of $\sum_k\lambda_k/(\lambda_k+\kappa_0)=n_0$, as the penalty vanishes. Even an infinitely repeated pilot of size $n_0$, observed at every penalty, therefore reveals the bias function $N(\kappa)$ only on the half-line $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$. We call $\kappa_0$ the *observability horizon* of the pilot. Modes with $\lambda_k\gg\kappa_0$ are resolved by the pilot; modes with $\lambda_k\ll\kappa_0$ are not.

**Proposition 1 (non-identifiability of the gain).** Let $(s,\sigma^2)$ be a target-power spectrum and noise variance, let $T$ be any set of modes with $\lambda_k\le\delta\kappa_0$ for some $\delta>0$, and let $S_T=\sum_{k\in T}s_k$. Define the alternative model $(s',\sigma'^2)$ with $s'_k=0$ for $k\in T$, $s'_k=s_k$ otherwise, and $\sigma'^2=\sigma^2+S_T$. Then

(a) for every penalty, the risk of a pilot of size $n_0$ differs between the two models by at most
$$0\le E'(n_0,\mu)-E(n_0,\mu)\le 2\delta\,S_T\,P(n_0,\kappa),\qquad(6)$$
i.e. by a relative amount of at most $2\delta S_T/N(\kappa_0)$;

(b) at a calibration size $n$ and any penalty for which $\kappa_n=\kappa(n,\mu)\le\delta'\min_{k\in T}\lambda_k$, the risks differ by at least
$$E'(n,\mu)-E(n,\mu)\ \ge\ (1-\delta'^2)\,S_T\,P(n,\kappa_n)\ \ge\ (1-\delta'^2)\,S_T.\qquad(7)$$

*Proof.* $N'(\kappa)-N(\kappa)=\sum_{k\in T}s_k\,[1-\kappa^2/(\lambda_k+\kappa)^2]=\sum_{k\in T}s_k\,\lambda_k(\lambda_k+2\kappa)/(\lambda_k+\kappa)^2$. Each summand lies in $[0,2\lambda_k/\kappa]$, because $\kappa\lambda_k(\lambda_k+2\kappa)\le2\lambda_k(\lambda_k+\kappa)^2$ for all $\lambda_k,\kappa\ge0$. For $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$ and $\lambda_k\le\delta\kappa_0$ this gives $0\le N'-N\le2\delta S_T$. The prefactor $P(n,\kappa)$ depends only on the eigenvalues, not on $(s,\sigma^2)$, and $\kappa(n,\mu)$ does not depend on them either; multiplying by $P(n_0,\kappa)$ gives (a). For (b), $\kappa_n/(\lambda_k+\kappa_n)\le\kappa_n/\lambda_k\le\delta'$ for $k\in T$, so each summand is at least $s_k(1-\delta'^2)$, and $P\ge1$. $\square$

Because $N'\ge N$ everywhere, the alternative model is also never better at its own optimal penalty, and in the numerical examples of Section 4.2 the difference in optimal risk is of the same order as $S_T$.

In words: target power hidden below the observability horizon looks like irreducible noise in the pilot's regularisation path (to first order in $\lambda_k/\kappa_0$), yet it is learned once the calibration is large enough for $\kappa_n$ to pass below those eigenvalues. The statement concerns the expected cross-validation path, which is the only information used by path-matching methods and, through the within-pilot learning curve, by curve extrapolation. Sampling noise in a cross-validated path (relative standard deviation of order $(2/n_0)^{1/2}$) makes the two models practically indistinguishable whenever $2\delta S_T/N(\kappa_0)$ is smaller than that noise. Because the gain from $n_0$ to $n$ is essentially the target power in the band $\kappa_n\lesssim\lambda_k\lesssim\kappa_0$, **a forecaster based on the pilot's error path cannot predict the gain without an assumption about how target power is distributed below the horizon.** Every published learning-curve extrapolation makes that assumption implicitly. The no-improvement rule assumes no power below the horizon. Inverse-power-law fits assume that the within-pilot trend continues. RPM with $r=1$ assumes power proportional to the eigenvalue, which puts almost none below the horizon. Estimators that use the full pilot data rather than its error path, such as the method-of-moments and EigenPrism estimators of the noise variance [@dicker2014; @janson2017], can in principle separate signal from noise without a prior on the signal. However, under a Gaussian design with known covariance, their standard error grows with $\sqrt{p}/n_0$, which is large for spectra with $p\gg n_0$. In our data such an estimator did not prevent noise-floor collapse (variant RPM-TD, Table 2). That weak modes act as effective noise for a given sample size is known from the theory of learning curves [@canatar2021; @hastie2022]. What Proposition 1 adds is the consequence for forecasting: it locates the horizon for a given pilot and turns it into a statement about the gain that a planner can check.

**Corollary (compatible set).** For a given parametric family of target-power spectra, the parameters whose predicted path is consistent with the observed pilot path, up to the sampling noise of the path, define a *compatible set* of learning curves. It is computable from the pilot and the unlabelled spectra alone. It is not an identification region in the strict sense: within a parametric family the parameters are identified in the noiseless limit, so the set's width reflects the precision that a pilot of size $n_0$ allows together with the bounds of the family. We report it for the family $s_k\propto(\lambda_k/\lambda_1)^r$, $r\in[0.05,1.6]$, with noise fraction $\eta\in[5\times10^{-4},0.97]$ (Section 2.5). In chemometric terms, $\kappa_0$ is the eigenvalue level at which a ridge model fitted to the pilot uses all $n_0$ effective degrees of freedom. Spectral directions whose variance lies well above it, roughly the leading principal components, are resolved by the pilot; directions well below it are not.

## 2.5. Bayesian regularisation-path matching (B-RPM)

Proposition 1 tells us where information must come from: the distribution of target power below the observability horizon. B-RPM supplies it as an explicit, transferable prior on *structural* parameters rather than on curve shapes. The bias function is parametrised as
$$N(\kappa)=V\left[\eta+(1-\eta)\,b_r(\kappa)\right],\qquad b_r(\kappa)=\frac{\sum_k(\lambda_k/\lambda_1)^r\,\kappa^2/(\lambda_k+\kappa)^2}{\sum_k(\lambda_k/\lambda_1)^r},\qquad(8)$$
where $V$ is the total variance ($N(\infty)=V$), $\eta$ the irreducible-noise fraction and $r$ the alignment exponent; the eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ are those of the task's own unlabelled spectra. The prior is Gaussian in $r$ and in $\mathrm{logit}\eta$, with location and scale (median and $1.4826\times$ median absolute deviation) taken from large-sample fits of previous calibrations, here all tasks of *other* data sources (leave-one-source-out) or, in a stricter test, of other application families. The likelihood is built from the pilot's exact leave-one-group-out path: log CV-MSE residuals have the delta-method variance of the log CV-MSE at the pilot optimum, and path points carry $\kappa$-quadrature weights scaled to $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=2$ effective observations (the level and the curvature of the path, since all points reuse the same residuals); $V$ is profiled out. The posterior on a $32\times40$ grid of $(r,\eta)$ is combined with the deterministic-equivalent optimal risk at every horizon to give a posterior-predictive distribution of $\log\mathrm{RMSEP}(n)$, to which the anchor uncertainty is added. All $\kappa$-dependent terms do not depend on $(r,\eta,V)$ and are precomputed, so a complete forecast with bands takes about 0.2 s. Two by-products are reported for every pilot: the prior-free compatible set (Corollary) and the *information fraction* $1-\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{post}}(\text{gain})/\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{prior}}(\text{gain})$, which measures how much the pilot itself adds to the prior about the future gain.

# 3. Materials and methods

## 3.1. Data sets

The study uses {{n_tasks}} calibration tasks from public sources (Table 1): six soil spectral libraries from the Open Soil Spectral Library v1.3 [@safanelli2025] (ICRAF-ISRIC VisNIR and MIR, KSSL MIR and VisNIR [@sanderman2020], AfSIS-1 MIR and a handheld NeoSpectra NIR set), each with organic carbon (OC), clay, pH(H$_2$O) and cation-exchange capacity (CEC) where available; dry-matter content of 4021 mango fruits measured with a handheld Vis–SWNIR instrument [@anderson2020; @mango_data]; six properties of diesel fuels (Southwest Research Institute NIR set); the active-ingredient assay of 655 tablets from the IDRC 2002 shoot-out; fat, moisture and protein of the Tecator meat data [@borggaard1992]; and four constituents of a designed casein–glucose–lactate–moisture (CGL) mixture set. OC and CEC were log-transformed ($\log(1+y)$) because of their skewness. Libraries with restrictive licences (LUCAS; the Serbian OSSL subset) were excluded. KSSL libraries were capped at 15,000 samples, sampled by whole sites.

## 3.2. Pre-processing and leakage control

Reflectance was converted to absorbance, and all spectra were standard-normal-variate (SNV) transformed [@barnes1989] after wavelength sub-sampling to 94–216 variables (Table 1). Both operations are sample-wise, so no information passes between calibration and test samples. Replicate scans of the same physical sample were averaged before any split. This applies to the two cheek scans of each mango fruit (fruit identity was reconstructed from metadata and validated by spectral distance), to duplicate KSSL scans, and to the multi-instrument NeoSpectra measurements, for which the instrument-averaged spectrum was used. Soil layers from the same site or profile were assigned to the same group. Every split is group-disjoint, and every cross-validation, inside pilots and inside ground-truth fits, leaves out whole groups [@kapoor2023]. Unlabelled spectra used by RPM and B-RPM were taken only from the calibration pool, never from the test set. Supplementary Note [[SN:DATA_LEAKAGE_AUDIT.md]] documents the full leakage audit.

## 3.3. Ground-truth learning curves

For each task, a group-disjoint test set (25% of samples, at most 3000) was held out using a seed derived from the task name; the remaining samples form the pool. Tasks with fewer than 1000 samples (fuel, tablets, meat, mixtures) were evaluated over ten independent group-disjoint outer splits, so that their ground truth does not rest on one small test set. For $n\in\{25,50,100,200,400,800,1600,3200,6400\}$ (up to the pool size), $n$ pool samples were drawn at random, and two calibrations were fitted. The first was ridge, with the penalty chosen by exact LOGO cross-validation over a 53-point grid. The second was PLS1 (kernel algorithm [@dayal1997]), with 1–30 latent variables chosen by grouped 10-fold cross-validation. The test MSE of each was recorded. The expected curve was estimated from 30 ($n\le400$), 16 ($n\le1600$) or 8 repetitions.

## 3.4. Pilot forecasting protocol

For pilot sizes $n_0\in\{25,50,100,200\}$, pilots were drawn at random from the pool with predefined seeds: 20 per task for the large libraries and 10 per outer split for the small data sets. Each forecaster received only the pilot (spectra, reference values, group labels), the unlabelled pool spectra and, where stated, information from *other* data sources. It forecast the test RMSEP at every grid size $n\ge2n_0$. The level endpoint is the absolute log ratio $|\log(\widehat{\mathrm{RMSEP}}/\mathrm{RMSEP}_{\mathrm{true}})|$ against the ridge ground truth; the gain endpoint is the absolute error of the forecast gain, $|\log(\widehat{\mathrm{RMSEP}}(n)/\widehat{\mathrm{RMSEP}}_{\mathrm{pilot}})-\log(\mathrm{RMSEP}_{\mathrm{true}}(n)/\mathrm{RMSEP}_{\mathrm{true}}(n_0))|$, which removes the noise of the pilot's own error estimate. Both are averaged within tasks (over splits, pilots, pilot sizes and horizons), so that every task counts once. Secondary endpoints are the signed bias by extrapolation ratio, the coverage and width of 80% and 95% bands, the coverage and width of the compatible set, and the Brier score of the probabilistic decision "an eightfold larger calibration set reduces RMSEP by at least 15%".

## 3.5. Forecasters

All forecasters use the same ridge calibration and the same pilot.

- **B-RPM** (Section 2.5), with the prior learned leave-one-source-out (LOSO) from the large-sample fits of the other data sources (sources defined by sample provenance; KSSL and NeoSpectra form one source), and **B-RPM-LOFO**, with the prior learned from other *application families* only. Ablations: prior on $r$ only (B-RPM-r), prior only without the pilot path (B-RPM-0) and $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=10$ (B-RPM-m10).
- **RPM** ($r=1$; the pre-specified method of the first design, Section 2.3), and its post-hoc variants with a transferred exponent (RPM-T) and noise floors (RPM-TD with the Dicker estimator [@dicker2014]; RPM-TP with a transferred lower bound).
- **IPL3**, an inverse power law with asymptote, $e(m)=a+bm^{-c}$, as used by Ali et al. [@ali2018] following [@mukherjee2003; @figueroa2012], fitted by weighted non-linear least squares to a within-pilot learning curve (training on 25–85% of the pilot, 20 group-disjoint splits per fraction) plus the full-pilot cross-validated error; **IPL2**, a pure power law; **EXP3** and **LOG2**, exponential and logarithmic curves.
- **WEI**, the scaling exponent of Wei et al. [@wei2022], estimated from the unlabelled eigen-decay and the pilot's regularisation path and anchored at the pilot error.
- **FLAT**, the pilot's cross-validated error (no improvement), and **DF-OLS**, a classical fixed-complexity formula.
- **SLOPE-T**, the pilot's cross-validated error extrapolated with the median ground-truth slope per doubling of $n$ of the other data sources, and **SLOPE-T-LOFO**, with the slope of other application families.

## 3.6. Design history, simulation and robustness

The study has three stages, all documented in a time-stamped experiment log. (1) The first design (RPM, baselines, ablations) was specified in a time-stamped protocol before any result was seen. (2) After an internal audit, the whole study was re-run with corrected soil groups, repeated outer splits for small data sets, provenance-based sources, a $\kappa$-quadrature weighting of the path fit and floor-free curve bands; first-version outputs are archived unchanged. (3) B-RPM, the compatible set and the hypotheses HB1–HB4 were specified and committed before any version-2 forecast was inspected: HB1, B-RPM-LOFO has a lower gain error than SLOPE-T-LOFO; HB2, B-RPM is non-inferior to FLAT on the level endpoint (margin 0.02); HB3, the 80% bands of B-RPM cover 70–90% in every family and pilot-size stratum; HB4, the compatible set contains the truth in at least 90% of cases. The leave-one-source-out recalibration of the B-RPM bands (Section 4.6) was added after HB3 had failed and is labelled post hoc.

**Simulation.** Gaussian spectra with $\lambda_k=k^{-1.6}$ ($p=150$), alignment $r\in\{0.5,1,2\}$ and noise fractions 0.1 and 0.3 were simulated; the ground truth is the Monte-Carlo test error of LOO-tuned ridge (30 repetitions, 4000 test samples), not the theory. B-RPM was given the real-data prior, which is approximately correct for $r=0.5$ and wrong for $r=2$.

**Robustness.** Six representative tasks were rerun with raw-absorbance and Savitzky–Golay first-derivative spectra [@savitzky1964], Kennard–Stone-selected pilots [@kennard1969] and Gaussian noise added to the reference values (variance 25% of $\mathrm{var}(y)$). Mango was also evaluated under a new harvest season (calibration on seasons 1–3, test on season 4, with an i.i.d. reference split of the same size) and with orchard populations held out.

## 3.7. Statistical analysis

The task is the unit of analysis. The pre-specified hypotheses HB1–HB4 are confirmatory and are reported with unadjusted p-values and confidence intervals; all other comparisons are exploratory. Paired comparisons use two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-task means [@wilcoxon1945], with Holm correction over the comparisons of a table, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals of the mean paired difference (4000 resamples of tasks). Because tasks share spectra within a source, every comparison is repeated at the level of the eight data sources (means over the tasks of a source); both levels are reported.

## 3.8. Implementation and reproducibility

All methods were implemented in Python (NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit-learn). The closed-form LOGO ridge path, the PLS1 implementation and the theory were unit-tested against brute-force refits, scikit-learn and Monte-Carlo simulation. A single command (`run_all.py`) downloads nothing silently, runs all experiments with deterministic seeds and regenerates every table, figure and number from machine-readable outputs. Computations ran on a 4-core CPU without a GPU.
